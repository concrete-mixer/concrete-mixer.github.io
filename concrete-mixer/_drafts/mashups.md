title: Bill McClintock

Mashups are a musical genre where a DJ or producer takes elements from two or more songs and combines them together. The intention is to amuse and delight. Mashups became a genre in the early 2000s, although similar crazes involving tacking disparate songs together sequentially date back to the 80s (think of horrors like Jive Bunny and Stars on 45). The older crazes were popularised by mass market, low rent music producers keen to make a quick buck; mashups on the other hand were a phenomenon of the internet, where youngsters on home computers could edit music sound files to combine songs. Mashups couldn't have happened earlier than the turn of the 21st Century, computer software and hardware only became sophisticated and ubiquitous enough at that time to make audio processing accessible.

While I enjoyed a small subset of mashups, a lot of the early ones were pretty bad. Producers were enthusiastic but did not always have an ear for key or rhythm, with awkward and unlistenable outcomes resulting. Some songs were blighted by digital adjustments to tempo or pitch that distorted originals too much to be successful. For all that there were good examples. One I remember fondly is a mashup of Missy Elliot's Get Ur Freak On with Joy Division's Love Will Tear Us Apart.

A crucial feature of mashups is humour, and the more incongruent the artists, the more comic potential. I remember a lot of early mashups could be characterised as "anything plus Eminem" (they were less sophisticated times...).

By the mid 2000s the first flowering of mashups ebbed. I'm guessing they didn't die out altogether, they just faded from prominence. I didn't pay any attention really until late 2020 when I discovered Bill McClintock.

Before we get to Bill, it's useful to survey how the technology has changed since the 2000s. Back then, the ability to separate out parts a music track where limited. If you were a song might have a capella or instrumental mixes published, and there might have been stripped back segments of a 12" mix to work with, but generally you were stuck with extracting elements from different sections of the stereo field, with cleanest results from hard left, right, and the centre. The centre is usually where the vocals are panned, but also the bass and kick drum, so there's getting a completely clean vocal was impossible, though with careful EQing you could filter out low and high frequency elements to some degree. Even so, mashup making was a process of continuous compromise.

(If you're wondering why I know so much about these things, it's because I used to make sampledelic electronica in the mid 2000s which, while not technically mashups, used a lot of the same techniques to nick bits from old songs.)

From about the mid-late 2010s, however, machine learning techniques to extract individual instruments and vocals became available, allowing songs to be split into reasonably clean "stems". With the stems of two different songs you can assemble essentially a new multitrack combining desired elements from each track. This enables mashups that sound more like authentic music and less like humourous but naff experiments.

Enter Bill. He's a middle school music teacher who has a hobbie making mashups. Not only does he have stems, he also has access to databases of songs which record key and tempo. Unlike 2000s mashers-up he's not focused on mashing up contemporary-but-incongruous chart toppers; instead he can identify a cluster of matching songs from all of history.

Bill's forte is something McClintock labels "Soul Metal", which either involves classic heavy metal vocals over a soul song, or the opposite, with a spurious metal guitar solo as extra garnish. Here's some examples: the vocals for The Temptations' Papa was a Rolling Stone with Danzig's Mother, The Isley Brothers' Shout with Motorhead's Ace of Spades, and James Brown's Soul Power vox with Rage Against the Machine's Killing in the Name. It's also clear that the added subtlety of picking songs similar rhythms in the same key, much higher quality results are obtained.

McClintock has produced dozens of mashups. My favourites usually have an element of the surreal to them. For example, Herb Alpert's schmaltzy "Rise" being paired with Black Sabbath's War Pigs, and Metallica's Master of Puppets. It's a giddy experience: the Ozzy Osbourne's ragged vocals and Hetfield's muscular shouting feel absurd over Alpert's "smooth" jazz funk arrangement.

Often McClintock will throw in guitar solos from other metal tracks. Because I'm not a metal head a lot of these aren't familiar to me, but I got a sense of what it would feel like to metalheads with a recent McClintock pairing of Peter Gabriel's Sledgehammer with Fly and the Family Stone's Thank You (For Letting Me Be Myself Again), after which came David Gilmour's solo to Another Brick in the Wall. It felt so apt, yet after hearing that guitar in a very different song for 45 years it felt very disorienting, maybe even thrillingly sacrilegious, to have it uplifted and placed against music that was less sour and more fun.

The vast majority of McClintock's songs are soul metal, and it's a well he consistently draws great mixes from. Frequent muses are Rick James, Metallica, Black Sabbath/Ozzy Osbourne, James Brown. Sometimes I think it would be interesting for McClintock to tackle other genres - there's worlds beyond both soul and metal. One tantalising example I like is a mashup of Young M.C.'s Bust a Move with Buffalo Springfield's For What it's Worth. However I think perhaps soul metal is already reasonably optimal: metal and soul aren't exactly inverses, but one is aiming for music that sound that is sweet and positive, while the other is trying to sound grim and majestic, and inherent contradiction in those aims is invariably going to make for a great mashup.

I could go on about McClintock's various masterpieces, but I'll limit myself to two - no, three! - all of which came out in 2025. I can't say for certain they're objectively any better than any others, but as I write they stick out in my mind.

The first features the vocals from Black Sabbath's Sabbath Bloody Sabbath over Britney Spears' Toxic. It works uncannily well. 

The second is based around Yes' Owner of a Lonely Heart. At first the vocal is the Dazz Band's Let it Whip, with occasional interjections from Devo's Whip It. So far pretty good, but as it feels like the song is coming to a conclusion, Ronnie James Dio bursts in with Rainbow in the Dark, his gigantic tenor taking everything up a gear. It's an experience that I can say, without any exaggeration, is magnificent.

The third mashup features the Rick James' You and I with Metallica's For Whom the Bell Tolls, followed by Ozzy Osbourne's Crazy Baby. The first thing that hooks me in is that the bell of one song is in tune with the other. It shouldn't be that surprising but I find it infinitely satisfying. Ozzy's input is pretty par for the course now with McClintock, but it's elevated with concert Hetfield's surprisingly upbeat audience engagement.

Are mashups art, music democratised, or just a bit of fun of not much import? Hard to say; but as long as we have digital technology, they're not going away. On the other hand, it's not an artform with legs. You can't make (to take a cliche example that will irritate foes of high European art) a Beethoven's Ninth of mashups. But all the same, a good mashup can invoke a surprising depth of feeling. I can thrill at how well James Brown sings to Motorhead; and enjoy the goofy charm of Glenn Danzig(?!) when he's paired with Donna Summer. And when I listen to Metallica sing One over Men at Work's Land Downunder (an absolutely inspired choice), I'm amused not so much by Hetfield's doomy lyrics over quasi reggae, but by the wry humour of Men at Work contrasted so starkly with Metallica's po-faced earnestness.

I've seen McClintock described as the "Mozart of mashups", and I say "yeah, why not?" I don't know if McClintock is a musical genius, but he's been adept, time and time again, at applying a simple formula that invariably work. But for copyright and rights headaches, his work could (and should, if there was any justice) make him a lot of money. But the music industry doesn't want to hand over control of music to everyone, and I guess I wouldn't either if I was the music industry.

Let's charge our glasses to Bill, a man who's brought much joy to tens of thousands of humans, which is more than can be said of most.
