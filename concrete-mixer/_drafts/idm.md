title: Autechre and IDM
---

I reached adulthood in the early 90s and it was a strange time of hopefulness. Geopolitically the Cold War ended, and the Doomsday Clock rolled back. A new era of technological connectedness had arrived. Not only were computers becoming cheap enough for every home to have one, there was now enough to do with that computer that justified the cost. No one was as eager to embrace the palpable but still nebulous future as me, getting online for the first time in 1994. Nothing was as exciting as talking to strangers on the other side of the world over IRC, or surfing the web with the text-based browser Lynx, on a server via a terminal.

Heady days indeed. At the time the "Intelligent Dance Music" or IDM groups were embracing the future on the English Warp Records label. Warp CDs proudly displayed email addresses and web domains at a time when only a few percent of the country was online. IDM was to mainstream dance music (at the time the "rave" was the staple genre in the UK). The "intelligent" in the name was intended to separate the music from that - it was intended to be a sound that your body could dance to if it wanted, but your brain would dance to it more.

Thanks to the Black Dog I came to associate IDM not only with "cyberspace", but with Egyptology and ancient alien contacts.

The Godfather of IDM was Aphex Twin. His 1992 album Selected Ambient Works '85 - '92 is - in my view - a manifesto for the genre. It was also his finest hour. Aphex Twin was labelled a genius by the music press, and indeed Richard D. James was a mercurial and talented producer. But his output was more about showing off and taking the piss than producing thoughtful works. Through the 90s he became less and less listenable, before dribbling to a stop in the 2000s. He released a comeback album in 2014 that proved to be return to the sound of 1992. The man has talent, but ultimately he proved to be a one trick pony.

I've devoted an essay to the Black Dog and Plaid, who were also bound up in IDM. I've tried listening to other luminaries like LFO, Squarepusher. The former I never managed to get into, and the latter just gave me a headache. Was Orbital IDM? Yeah I suppose, although they were a bit more mainstream. Future Sound of London were certainly more pretentious. I liked the singles Papua New Guinea and Cascade, but their much lauded 1994 ambient double album Lifeforms left me completely cold, being two hours of unfocused pissing about.

I didn't get into Autechre until a few years later, but they proved to be the "real deal" of IDM. The duo (these groups seemed to work best as duos) hailed from Rochdale in Lancashire, and had a real talent for going deep into a sound. Their first album, Incunabula from 1993, was a reasonably recognisable piece of techno, serving as a launchpad for more experimental work.

(As an aside I didn't buy Incunabula until 2001, around the time of 9/11. Thus the acid burbles and bleak chords of the albums opening track are now my soundtrack to that atrocity.)

I first heard Autechre's second album Amber in 1995. The track Foil sounded absolutely immense and abstract and had a big effect on me. It was such a harsh and out there sound. The rest of the album doesn't reach the same heights, but there are several other great tracks.

As well as LPs, Autechre also frequently released EPs. The most impressive of these are the Garbage and Anvil Vapre EPs, released between Amber and the next LP Tri Repetae. The chart a change of sound, from human to alien with increasing bleakness. Tri Repetae seemed to complete a process of transformation from human techno to something quite alien and other.

Unfortunately Tri Repetae marks the last Autechre album I can listen all the way through without giving up. 1997's Chiastic Slide and 1998's LP5 have some good moments, but increasingly the sound was becoming too alien and unrewarding of listening attention. (Interestingly the EPs from the period are a bit more listenable.) For a long time I believed that Autechre had disappeared up its own arse, becoming wilfully difficult and worthy only from attention to those listeners who embrace the EXTREME. In more recent times I've come to see "difficult" Autechre as the result of a creative practice that never lost its authenticity or desire to delve ever further into the fundamentals of sound, but which passed beyond my event horizon of listenability.
